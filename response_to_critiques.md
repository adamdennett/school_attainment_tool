# Response to critiques of *How to Pull the Right Lever*

## Before the point-by-point: context, and what was actually objected to

Two things frame everything that follows.

**How this critique reached the public.** The academic criticisms answered below did not arrive as unsolicited scholarly comment. By Class Divide's own account — in a letter circulated to councillors on the evening of the report's launch — the campaign "asked a group of respected education professors to review" the report, and attached their responses as an appendix to its own reply. That is entirely the campaign's right. But it means these critiques were commissioned and deployed by a group with a declared position, as one instrument of a lobbying effort aimed at elected members, rather than offered as neutral review. I engage them fully on their merits regardless. But anyone weighing them deserves to know that context — the more so because the same letter dismisses my report for not being "peer-reviewed," while presenting, as its own evidence, reviews it solicited from parties who were never disinterested. (For the record: a version of the report is under peer review at a journal now. But the deeper answer is the one this whole project is built on. Peer review is a proxy for scrutiny — the checking of work by people who did not do it. My report offers a more searching version of exactly that: every figure is built from data the government publishes for every school in England, with the methods and code open for the council, for Class Divide, or for any professor to rerun, challenge or overturn. Two anonymous referees are a narrow test; "anyone can check the whole thing" is a wider one. And the findings do not rest on my say-so in any case: they reproduce a consensus the field reached decades before I looked at it.)

**What was actually objected to.** Both the campaign and its commissioned critics rest on a picture of the objection that is mistaken, and the mistake matters. Having declared that "no one loses" from the reform, Professor Gorard allows exactly one exception to it — that "some parents in Brighton and Hove may feel concerned about their children having to attend with a refreshed intake." That is the *whole* of the downside his account entertains; displacement, longer journeys and lost attendance do not appear in it at all. The campaign, similarly, casts the resistance as wealthier families reluctant to see poorer children arrive. Neither is what was argued. Brighton and Hove's schools are already among the most socially mixed in the country, and the families who objected already send their children to those mixed schools — the objection was never to who might come *in*. It was to children being denied a place at their own local school, on the promise that displacing them would benefit others: a promise the evidence does not support. Had the reforms let children travel in from elsewhere *without* pushing local children out, there would have been little argument at all. Recasting a principled objection to displacement as mere discomfort at a "refreshed intake" does not meet that objection — it changes the subject.

**On the charge that this is "news in Whitehawk."** The campaign's sharpest factual point is that the report claims our children "are already doing well" — and that "would be news in Whitehawk," where for years only around three in ten have left school with passes in English and maths. This deserves a straight answer. I do not dispute the figure, and I would never stand in front of that community and call its children's outcomes acceptable: they are not, and the poverty behind them is real and unjust. But the report does not say those outcomes are fine. It says something different, and more useful. Raw results like "three in ten" measure, overwhelmingly, the circumstances children bring with them, not the quality of the school that receives them. Once you account for the intake a school actually teaches, the schools serving our most disadvantaged communities are not failing — several add more value than schools with far easier intakes, and BACA, in the city's deprived north-east, is by this measure among the strongest schools in the country for disadvantaged pupils. Low attainment and a weak school are not the same thing, and the whole purpose of the report is to stop us confusing them. This is not word-play, and it is certainly not complacency; it is the difference between a diagnosis that leads somewhere and one that does not. If Whitehawk's results were the school's own doing, moving children between schools might fix them. Because they overwhelmingly are not, it will not — which is exactly why the effort belongs with attendance, and with the poverty that drives it. Read properly, the Whitehawk figure is not a refutation of the report; it is the report's argument, in a single statistic.

I will not dwell on the parts of this that are about me rather than the work — the postcode ("Preston Park parent"), the title (a professorship described as "a misuse of power"). They make the same move in opposite directions: one implies that where I live should discredit what I find, the other that my job should lend it too much weight. Both have the same answer, and it is the one the whole report is designed around. You need not trust a professor, or wonder about his address, because the data are public, the code is open, and the result is one you can reproduce for yourself. An argument built so that anyone can rebuild it is not an appeal to authority — it is the deliberate refusal of one.

None of this lessens my willingness to meet the critiques on their merits, in good faith and in detail — which is what the rest of this document does.

I am grateful to Professor Gorard and Professor Reay for engaging so seriously with the report. Their critiques are substantial, and several sharpen the argument in ways I welcome. In the spirit of the report — which argues for better public reasoning from open data, not for any single answer — I want to respond point by point, conceding where they are right, clarifying where I think the report has been read for more than it claims, and engaging honestly where we genuinely disagree.

Two prefatory points frame everything below. First, the report's central contribution is not the claim "absence matters more than disadvantage." It is that the Department for Education's open data can be turned into accessible, contextualised intelligence for local decision-makers, and that in one live case (Brighton and Hove, 2024–25) the absence of such intelligence contributed to a policy designed around a partial reading of the evidence. Second, the report repeatedly states that its models measure *attainment outcomes only*, and cannot speak to the broader social, civic and developmental value of diverse school communities. Several of the criticisms below are, I think, best understood as asking me to foreground that caveat more than I did — a request I largely accept.

## Areas of agreement

It is worth beginning with how much even the report's critics accept, because the disagreements are narrower than a first reading might suggest. On the factual landscape there is, I think, substantial common ground.

Professor Gorard confirms the two facts about Brighton and Hove that mattered most to the 2024 debate and were least present in it: that the city's between-school segregation "is not remarkable, and is known to be worse elsewhere," and that "the attainment gap in Brighton and Hove is also better (smaller) than in many other areas of England." These were precisely the contextual facts absent from the consultation, and I am glad they are not in dispute. Both critics affirm that the attainment gap is multi-causal — Professor Gorard notes it is "well-known that the attainment gap has no single cause," which is exactly the report's position — and that poverty is foundational to it, shaping outcomes from the early years onward. Both accept, relatedly, that prior attainment reflects accumulated earlier inequality; indeed Professor Gorard's argument that it is a proxy for early poverty is a sharper statement of the report's own claim that Key Stage 4 outcomes carry forward inequalities from well before secondary school. And neither critic argues against reducing absence: Professor Gorard is explicit that his point is "not an argument for not trying to reduce absence as well."

Beyond these, several of the report's foundations went unchallenged by either critic: that Department for Education open data can be turned into accessible, contextualised intelligence for local decision-makers (the report's central purpose); that contextualised benchmarking is a fairer basis for judging schools than raw league tables or single-word inspection grades — a view I take Professor Gorard to share, given his own long-standing work on the unreliability of school rankings; and that school-level attainment is, descriptively, highly predictable from a small set of open variables.

I want to be careful not to claim more agreement than there is. Concurring on the facts is not concurring on their interpretation or on what should follow: Professor Gorard reads Brighton and Hove's smaller gap as a *consequence* of its greater integration, and therefore as a reason to integrate further, whereas the report questions the size of the specific *attainment* benefit that further redistribution would bring to disadvantaged pupils. That is a real disagreement, and I turn to it below. But it is a disagreement about interpretation and policy, resting on a shared understanding of the factual landscape — and that shared understanding is a better starting point for the argument than is sometimes assumed.

## Responses to Professor Gorard

**On segregation levels in Brighton and Hove.** I agree entirely, and so does the report. Brighton and Hove is consistently in the top third of English local authorities for the integration of disadvantaged pupils across its schools — the report says exactly this, using your own segregation index. I also agree this is no argument for complacency, and that many authorities, especially rural and low-density ones, face worse segregation and should act. Nothing in the report claims novelty on this point; it draws on your established work precisely because it is established.

**On the attainment gap having no single cause.** We are in full agreement, and I would not want to be read as suggesting otherwise. The report's explicit position is that attainment is shaped by "multiple, interacting, non-linear and context-dependent" factors. If the "pull the right lever" framing implied single causation to any reader, that is a failure of framing on my part, not of analysis: the intended meaning is narrower — that among many genuine causes, a specific *local* decision (the 2024 admissions proposal) was weighted toward a factor with comparatively little mechanical leverage for its stated goal, while a factor with more went undiscussed. I take the point that the metaphor invites the reductive reading, and I will guard against it.

**On prior attainment as a proxy for early poverty.** This is the criticism I most accept, and it improves the work. Key Stage 2 prior attainment is indeed, in substantial part, accumulated family advantage and disadvantage; conditioning on it absorbs much of the poverty signal, and a disadvantage coefficient estimated *after* that control will understate the total role of poverty. The report treats prior attainment as an *intake control* — the models ask "given the pupils a school receives, what is associated with their outcomes?" — and not as a claimed lever. But you are right that presenting the post-control disadvantage coefficient without foregrounding what has been absorbed risks misleading. The correct remedy, which I will adopt, is to report the disadvantage association both with and without prior attainment in the model, so that the absorption is visible rather than buried, and to state plainly that prior attainment is downstream of the very poverty whose contemporaneous effect it partly conceals.

**On coefficients versus effect sizes.** This is where I most strongly push back, because I think the report has been read for something it does not do. The models are not high-coefficient/low-R² models: they explain roughly 80% of the variation in school-level Attainment 8. And the report does not rest on coefficients alone — it translates them into Attainment 8 points through explicit scenarios (bringing the worst-affected schools to the national average for absence yields an estimated 3–5 GCSE points; even large reductions in disadvantage concentration yield 1–2, and crucially not for disadvantaged pupils themselves). That is effect-size reasoning, not coefficient-reading. I do accept the narrower and legitimate version of your point: a large *standardised* coefficient for a factor affecting a minority of pupils does not equal that factor's aggregate impact on the population gap, and I should make the distinction explicit. I am happy to add the incremental (unique) variance attributable to absence to the tables, which speaks directly to this.

**On the magnitude of the school effect.** It is worth stating plainly how large the school effect actually is, because the whole balance of the Brighton and Hove argument turns on it — and here the report is on the firmest possible ground, because it simply recovers the settled international consensus. The school-effectiveness literature has for decades put the share of variation in pupil outcomes attributable to differences *between* schools at 8–15 per cent: Smith and Tomlinson's landmark English study, *The School Effect* (1989), settled on around 10 per cent across all tests and groups, and Teddlie and Reynolds' *International Handbook of School Effectiveness Research* (2000) confirms that range across countries and phases (with the balance of variance attributable to pupil intake and social context — the Coleman and Jencks inheritance). My own decomposition recovers exactly this: the persistent, unexplained between-school component is 10.5 per cent of the variance for disadvantaged pupils, and 15.6 per cent across all pupils. Translated into the currency of the Brighton debate, this is decisive. The disadvantage gap in Attainment 8 is about twelve points; the standard deviation of the persistent school effect for disadvantaged pupils is about 2.7 points. So moving a disadvantaged child from an average school to a genuinely good one (the top quarter) is worth on the order of two Attainment 8 points, and to an excellent one (the top tenth) about three and a half — a quarter to a third of the gap at the very most. Even the theoretical ceiling — lifting a child from the *worst* school in the distribution to the *best* — reaches only about nine points, three-quarters of the gap; and that figure is itself an over-estimate, because the persistent between-school component still bundles in unmeasured pupil sorting that no school-level model can fully strip out, so the genuine causal effect of the building and its teaching is smaller still. This is not a quibble about coefficients: it is the cross-national, half-century-old magnitude of the school effect, expressed in GCSE points, and it is precisely why the report concludes that redistributing pupils between the schools of an already-integrated city is a weak lever for those pupils' attainment. It also, I think, reinforces the previous point: the very fact that this magnitude is so well established is what makes it robust to any objection about *this* study's methods.

**On non-linearity.** I think there has been a misreading here that I can clear up straightforwardly. The report does not assess only linear relationships. It log-transforms several predictors, fits natural-spline specifications, and devotes a section ("accelerating returns") entirely to the non-linear point — that the same percentage-point change in absence yields very different attainment impacts depending on where a school sits on the distribution. The log-log bivariate analyses and the spline models are in the modelling section and its supplement. So I agree with the general principle you state — that a low *linear* effect size need not mean two variables are unrelated — but in this case the report already acts on it. I would welcome any view on whether the non-linear specifications go far enough.

**On significance tests with population data.** This is the objection on which the critique tries hardest to plant a flag, so let me deal with it in full. First, the concession it deserves: significance testing *is* widely misused in social science, p-values are routinely over-read, and I have no wish to defend that — the American Statistical Association said as much in its 2016 statement (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016), and I agree. But that reasonable point is not what is being asserted here. What is asserted is the absolutist version — that because these are population data rather than a random sample, the inference is "mathematically meaningless," and the work can therefore be set aside. On that, four things belong on the record.

First, this is not a critique of my report. It is a general position Professor Gorard has pressed against essentially the whole of quantitative social science for the better part of two decades, in the same terms each time — most vividly in a paper in which he writes that anyone who "continues to teach, use or publish significance tests is acting unethically, and knowingly risking the damage that ensues," and describes his colleagues' practice as "dangerous obstinacy" (Gorard, 2016). An objection levelled identically at an entire discipline, whatever study happens to be in front of it, cannot tell a councillor — or anyone else — anything about *this* study in particular.

Second, it is not the settled truth it is presented as, but a minority position that his own field has answered, directly and repeatedly, in print. Writing in the *Oxford Review of Education*, Dave Neale (Cambridge) observes that Professor Gorard "presents one view on a long-running debate … as if the matter has been settled … and educational researchers simply need to 'come to their senses' and realise the truth" — before showing why the central claim fails: "a sample is real information about a population — and, in fact, that is the whole point of a sample" (Neale, 2015). Nicholson and McCusker (Durham — his own institution — and Northumbria) note that the sweeping charge of unethical practice "impugns the integrity, not just the competence, of a large number of highly respected academics," and that the key demonstration in Gorard's own paper "uses a sample size which is far too small" and "misrepresents what hypothesis tests claim to do" (Nicholson & McCusker, 2016). Kuha and Sturgis (LSE) show that the alternative he offers in place of significance testing is itself flawed and "risks leading researchers to draw incorrect conclusions" (Kuha & Sturgis, 2016). This is not a stray reply or two: it runs from a 2014 symposium of responses (Glass, Howe, Putwain, Van Daal & Adèr, White) through 2015 and 2016, with Professor Gorard restating the position again in 2017. To present the objection to a lay committee as though it were the consensus of statistics — while omitting that statistics has answered it, at length and more than once — is not a neutral account of where the argument stands.

Third, and most simply: if the absolutist claim were true, most of modern quantitative social science would disappear with it. As Neale puts it, accepting it "would mean that no research … could ever generalise, in any way whatsoever, to any individuals beyond those involved." Over half of all administrative-data studies in the leading US education journals use exactly the inference in question (Gibbs, Shafer & Miles, 2017); so does most census-based social science, most panel-data econometrics, much of the analytical output of national statistical offices, and the whole of registry-based epidemiology. One is asked to believe that this entire enterprise, built and maintained by thousands of professional statisticians over decades, rests on an elementary error that Professor Gorard alone has seen through. I do not believe it, and — the published record shows — neither does his field.

Fourth, the report does not merely assume inference is licensed here; it displays the very randomness that licenses it. School-level scores swing markedly from year to year — a lag-1 correlation of about 0.61 at Key Stage 2 against 0.95 at Key Stage 4 — and that cohort-to-cohort instability is precisely the stochastic variation a standard error is built to capture. The multilevel shrinkage the models apply is, moreover, the standard protection against over-reading small schools' noisy single-year scores — the very over-interpretation Professor Gorard has, rightly, warned against for years. So the machinery he objects to is doing work he should welcome.

And in the end it changes nothing here. The report's conclusions do not rest on any p-value. They rest on effect sizes translated into GCSE points, on replication across four separate years, on that shrinkage, and above all on recovering — from open data anyone can rerun — a result the field has established on independent grounds since the 1960s. Delete every significance test in the report and the argument stands unchanged. Which is, in the end, the most telling thing about the objection: pressed to its limit, it removes nothing from what the report actually concludes.

If the work has drawn this particular objection, I can at least take some comfort in the company: Professor Gorard has aimed the same argument, in the same words, at a large part of his own discipline for twenty years. It is less a verdict on any single piece of research than a standing position — and one his fellow statisticians have answered many times over.

**On the tension between "already established" and "methodologically unsound."** There is, I think, a real tension in holding both that the report's findings are well-known and already established, and that the methods producing them are unsound. The two claims pull against each other, for a straightforward reason: if a finding is well established across the wider literature, then it is by definition robust to the methodology of any single study — including this one — and cannot stand or fall on this study's significance tests or choice of model. So the "it is already known" observation, which I accept, works to *defend* these findings against the methodological critique rather than to compound it: whatever the general merits of the objection to inference on population data, it cannot touch conclusions the field already supports on other grounds. And there is a second point in the same direction. A method that recovers the established consensus — from open data that anyone can re-run — while adding the local and cross-phase detail that raw differences alone cannot provide, is doing precisely what it is meant to do. That is a reason to regard the multilevel machinery as useful, not "needless." I do not take this to settle the wider methodological argument, which is a genuine and old one; I take it only to show that the "already established" concession disarms the inferential critique as it applies to what the report actually concludes. (I keep this separate from the prior-attainment point above, which is a fair criticism of *interpretation* and which I accept, not a criticism of the inferential apparatus.)

**On causal ordering and the malleability of levers.** I agree that causal order matters, and that family structure, parental education, health and the early clustering of poverty precede prior attainment — which is part of why I accept the proxy point above. I also accept the pragmatic force of the malleability argument: short-term, within-education actionability is a legitimate criterion, and admissions arrangements are more immediately adjustable than family poverty. Where we differ is empirical, not methodological. My analysis suggests that the *mechanical attainment* benefit of de-clustering, specifically for disadvantaged pupils, is small — and it is that specific quantity, not the fairness of the intake, on which the 2024 proposal rested its educational case.

It is on "no one loses" that I must respectfully but firmly disagree, because the report sets out at length why the claim does not hold — and, importantly, it does not hold *on Professor Gorard's own terms*. De-clustering an already-integrated authority necessarily lengthens journeys: to redistribute pupils away from their local schools is, by construction, to send more of them further each day. The report devotes a section of its literature review to the consequences of this, and the evidence is not thin. The most direct runs through absence, the very factor Professor Gorard agrees is worth reducing: using English National Pupil Database data, Thomson (2023) shows that pupils with longer journeys to school are absent more often, with the effect concentrated among already-disadvantaged groups — a finding echoed in natural-experiment and cross-national work (Cordes et al. 2022; Blagg et al. 2018; Otsuka 2025). So the policy that Professor Gorard commends plausibly *increases* absence for exactly the pupils it means to help, and since we both accept that absence depresses attainment, the "almost no cost" framing is self-undermining: even a small induced rise in disadvantaged-pupil absence would erode, and could exceed, any attainment gain from a more mixed intake. Beyond absence, the report documents a wider set of costs that fall unevenly on the already-marginalised (Shah 2023): reduced sleep and poorer sleep quality (Fredriksen & Rhodes 2004; Yeo et al. 2019); the loss of the active travel that supports physical and mental health (Faulkner et al. 2013; Voulgaris et al. 2019); elevated stress and lower wellbeing; and the weakening of schools as walked-to community anchors — the friendship groups that map onto catchments, and the parental engagement that depends on proximity. "No one loses" is therefore not a neutral description; it is a strong empirical claim that the report gives specific, cited reasons to reject.

None of this is an argument against integration where segregation is genuinely high — it is an argument that in an already-integrated city the costs of further redistribution are real, borne disproportionately by disadvantaged families, and capable of undoing the very attainment benefit the policy seeks. That is a testable proposition, and I share Professor Gorard's interest in the answer: I would strongly welcome a proper evaluation of the Brighton and Hove changes as they take effect — tracking journey lengths, absence and attainment for displaced pupils — as exactly the natural experiment that could move the question from assertion to evidence, on both sides.

**A closing note, joining the two halves of this response.** There is a final irony worth drawing out. Professor Gorard titled his 2016 methodological paper "Damaging Real Lives Through Obstinacy." It is a phrase I would gently return to him. The claim on which his advice to the council actually rested — that in redistributing pupils between schools "no one loses" — is the one that risks damaging real lives: the children sent further from their local schools, whose attendance, wellbeing and sense of belonging the evidence suggests will suffer for it. The obstinacy that ought to concern us is not a discipline's continued, careful use of a standard statistical tool; it is the insistence, against the evidence, that a policy carrying real and unequally distributed costs somehow carries none.

## Responses to Professor Reay

**On the interconnection of the factors.** I accept the substance of this, and it is the criticism that most reshapes how the findings should be presented. The report separates absence, prior attainment, disadvantage and segregation for analytical tractability, but they are not independent, and I should not let the presentation imply otherwise. Poverty patterns absence — the report's own residence-based data show a steep gradient, with children from the most deprived neighbourhoods absent far more — and absence in turn depresses attainment. What the models can show is narrower than independence and narrower than a claim that absence is poverty-free: they show that the association between absence and attainment *survives* conditioning on disadvantage, i.e. that there is signal beyond what disadvantage alone explains. That is a defensible and, I think, useful finding. It is not a claim that attendance can be raised without addressing its causes, and I agree that attendance interventions divorced from the poverty, ill-health, unmet special educational need and school experiences that drive non-attendance are likely to disappoint. I would only add that the empirical question — how much attendance is movable, and how durably — is open, and I would not want either "it is a lever" or "gains are minimal and temporary" to be treated as established without evidence.

**On the catchment system concentrating privilege.** There is a kernel here I accept: popular schools attract more advantaged families, in Brighton and Hove as everywhere. But on the specific claim that the *catchment system concentrates* privilege, my own analysis points the other way. Mapping postcode-level deprivation onto catchment composition shows the city's catchments doing a great deal of work to *even out* concentrations of disadvantage across its schools — they are a substantial part of *why* Brighton and Hove is more integrated than most places, rather than a mechanism of segregation ([evidence here](https://adamdennett.github.io/BH_Schools_Consultation/postcode_school_pop.html)). I would keep this carefully distinct from a separate and entirely legitimate question — that of *access*: whether every family had a fair *choice* of school. That is not the same as how deprivation is distributed once children are placed, my analysis speaks to the latter, and I do not take the composition finding to settle the fairness of access.

**On the reframing of a political question as a technical one.** This is the deepest criticism and I accept it most fully. How school places should be allocated, and whom schools should serve, is a question of justice and of the kind of community we want, and it cannot be settled by a coefficient. The report tries to hold this line explicitly — it states that its models speak to attainment outcomes only and cannot adjudicate the civic and developmental value of mixed intakes, and that its findings should not be read as an argument against integration in general. But I take the point that a report organised around "which lever moves attainment" inevitably foregrounds the technical and risks crowding out the ethical, however many caveats it carries. My defence is narrow and I will state it narrowly: the 2024 proposal made a specific *empirical* claim — that redistributing disadvantaged pupils would narrow their attainment gap — and it is legitimate, and I think important, to test that specific claim on the evidence. What the evidence cannot do, and what I do not ask it to do, is decide whether a fairer intake is worth having for its own sake. On that, I agree with you: it is a political and ethical question, and data should inform it without pretending to resolve it.

## Common ground

I will end where I think all three of us in fact agree. Segregation between schools is real, is worse in many places than in Brighton and Hove, and is worth addressing. The attainment gap has many interacting causes, of which poverty is foundational. School-level metrics are noisy and easily over-read, and public reasoning about them should be more careful than it usually is. And the allocation of school places is ultimately a question of fairness that evidence should inform but not usurp. Where I continue to differ is on one specific empirical claim — the mechanical attainment benefit of de-clustering for disadvantaged pupils — and on the interpretation of inference from population data. Both of those are testable, and I would rather see them tested than asserted. I am grateful for critiques that make the testing sharper.

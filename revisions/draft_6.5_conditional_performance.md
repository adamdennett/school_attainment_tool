# Draft revision — §6.5

Replaces the current **"What is driving Brighton and Hove's over-achievement? And
what we don't yet know"** section. Addresses **R2.7**, is the natural home for the
full "cost of absence" argument, and carries the strategic reframe (**R1.2**-adjacent).

Written to be consistent with the §5.2 draft and with §5.5 (so it does not
re-trigger the R2.8 tension). Prose for review, not yet in the paper.

---

## Proposed new title

> ## Conditional performance and the cost of absence

Rationale: the current title asserts "over-achievement" as established fact, which
is exactly the claim R2.7 shows to be conditional. The new title names the two
things the section actually does.

## Draft prose

> The local-authority effect for Brighton and Hove is positive and, in the main
> model, statistically significant across all three attainment groups. As §5.2
> set out, however, this result is conditional on how the model treats absence,
> and the city's attendance is so extreme that the conditioning does most of the
> work. Controlling for absence, the city's schools rank 7th of 151 for
> disadvantaged attainment; without that control they rank 46th and are
> statistically ordinary. We take neither number as the whole truth. Together
> they say something more useful than either alone: **Brighton and Hove's schools
> appear to be genuinely effective for the pupils who attend them, and the city's
> attendance problem is large enough to erase that advantage in the aggregate.**
>
> The distance between the two rankings has a concrete interpretation. It is
> roughly 1.3 Attainment 8 points per disadvantaged pupil --- the difference
> between what the city's disadvantaged children achieve and what they might
> achieve if their schools' underlying effectiveness were not being drained by
> absence. That figure is a lower bound on the stakes, not an upper one: it is the
> penalty already visible in the aggregate LA effect, before any account of how
> non-linear returns to attendance concentrate the losses in the city's
> worst-attending schools (§5.3). Read this way, the city's attendance is not a
> caveat to a good-news story. It is the story.
>
> This reframes what the 2024 consultation got right and what it got wrong. The
> council was not wrong that Brighton and Hove has a problem: unconditionally, its
> disadvantaged pupils do no better than the national average, and the raw
> attainment gap that motivated the consultation is real. What the consultation
> got wrong was the diagnosis. Presented with an attainment gap, it reached for
> the social composition of schools --- one of the weakest levers our modelling
> identifies, and for disadvantaged pupils a lever that may point the wrong way
> (§4.3, §5.3). Meanwhile the factor that actually separates the city's
> conditional standing from its unconditional standing --- attendance --- was
> absent from the consultation entirely. The more accurate account is not that a
> high-performing city was mistaken for a failing one, but that a city with one
> specific, severe, addressable problem was prescribed a treatment for a
> different problem it did not have.
>
> That the problem is specific and local, rather than an inevitability of the
> city's intake, is what makes it addressable. Brighton and Hove's disadvantage is
> close to the national average --- its mean school free-school-meal rate is
> marginally below it --- so its absence cannot be explained away as a consequence
> of a deprived intake. When absence is modelled from intake alone (§5.5), the
> city's schools should record around 8.8% absence; they record 10.8%. And where
> almost every other authority reduced absence as attendance recovered from the
> pandemic --- 94% of local authorities improved between 2021--22 and 2024--25 ---
> Brighton and Hove's did not move. The city became a national outlier not because
> its absence rose but because, nearly alone, its absence failed to fall. This is
> the profile of a problem that sits within the reach of local and system action,
> which is precisely why we treat it, and not admissions reform, as the city's
> most valuable lever.
>
> None of this tells us *why* the city's schools are as effective as the
> conditional result suggests. The local-authority effect, by construction,
> captures variance the fixed-effect predictors do not explain. It could reflect
> the city's well-educated population and strong civic culture, the quality of
> school leadership and governance, effective local authority support and school
> improvement work, or collaborative relationships between schools that are
> difficult to quantify. Identifying the sources of this conditional
> over-performance is an important question in its own right, and one that would
> likely require qualitative investigation or pupil-level data beyond what is
> publicly available. What is clear is the asymmetry of risk: the city's
> conditional effectiveness is real but unexplained, while its attendance problem
> is both measurable and, on the evidence here, the largest single constraint on
> what its disadvantaged pupils achieve. Policy that disrupts the former in
> pursuit of a composition effect our models do not support, while leaving the
> latter unaddressed, would be pulling the wrong lever in the fullest sense.

## Notes on the draft

1. **The "right about the problem, wrong about the cause" reframe is now baked
   in** (paragraph 3). This is the strategic shift flagged in the §5.2 draft. If
   you want to keep the softer "high-performing city mistaken for a failure"
   framing instead, paragraph 3 is where to change it — but note it would then
   sit in tension with the unconditional 46th, which paragraph 1 has just stated.
   The two need to agree.

2. **Consistency with §5.5 / R2.8.** The draft deliberately never says "the
   council failed to prevent this absence" or "the schools cause the absence." It
   says the absence is *addressable* and *local*, and locates the manageable
   portion via the intake-only comparison (the excess over intake prediction) —
   which is exactly the §5.5 decomposition logic. This keeps §6.5 consistent with
   the decomposition's finding that only part of absence is school-controllable.
   When you draft R2.8, keep this alignment.

3. **"1.3 points as a lower bound."** I've framed the gap as a lower bound because
   the accelerating-returns analysis (§5.3) shows the losses concentrate
   non-linearly in the worst-attending schools, so the aggregate LA-effect gap
   understates the per-school stakes at the extreme. Check you're comfortable
   asserting "lower bound" — it's defensible but it is a claim.

4. **Keeps the useful content from the current section.** The "what we don't yet
   know" material (parental engagement, leadership, civic culture, need for
   qualitative / pupil-level follow-up) is preserved in paragraph 5, just
   reframed from "over-achievement" to "conditional over-performance."

5. **Ends on the paper's title.** "Pulling the wrong lever in the fullest sense"
   ties the section back to the framing device. Drop if it reads as too neat.

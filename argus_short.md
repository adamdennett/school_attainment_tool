---

Last week I gave a public talk at BHASVIC about what really drives secondary school attainment, and what it means for our city. It followed two bruising years of consultation and reform over school admissions. I opened with the question any policy like this has to answer: do the benefits outweigh the costs?

It sounds abstract. It isn't. When Brighton and Hove reformed admissions, that judgement was implicit in the policy, even if it was never stated so plainly. On one side of the scales sat a cost: some children would no longer get a place at their nearest catchment school. On the other sat a benefit: some disadvantaged children would win the "golden ticket" — a place at a sought-after central school that would lift their results. The council weighed the two and decided the benefit was worth the cost. That is a legitimate way to make a decision. But a scale only balances if one side is genuinely heavy enough to justify the other. And the benefits were never quantified, despite the data being there to do it, while the costs were never fully acknowledged. We never really knew.

My report is an attempt to weigh them. Using the Government's open data on every school in England, you can model what actually shapes how well a child does by the end of secondary school. The finding is a humbling one: the school a child attends explains only a small slice of the GCSE results they leave with — around 10 to 15 per cent. The rest is set long before the school gates, by family, by poverty, by the neighbourhood a child grows up in. This is not a fringe idea. Researchers have found it again and again since the landmark Coleman report in 1960s America, and through decades of school effectiveness research in Britain.

Which means our raw league tables badly mislead us about what a good school is. Schools with high headline scores mostly have them because of who they teach, not how they teach. And some genuinely excellent schools, whose pupils exceed expectations by a wide margin, are maligned for low headline numbers. BACA in north-east Brighton has been one of them — though I am glad to see perceptions changing fast.

So the golden ticket is, for the most part, a mirage. Moving children between schools does not change who they are or where they come from, and that is what matters above everything else.

Put it in plain numbers. The gap between disadvantaged children and the rest is about 12 to 15 points on Attainment 8, the score that adds up a child's GCSE grades. My report puts the average gain from reshuffling pupils to even out disadvantage — the lever the council chose — at perhaps one to two of those points, and, tellingly, not for the disadvantaged children it was meant to help.

Now the alternative. By far the biggest factor holding back disadvantaged pupils is absence — and last year Brighton and Hove had the second worst absence in England. Lifting this city's attendance to the level of its own best-attended school would be worth around five GCSE points for disadvantaged children, without moving a single one of them. Shuffling children between schools does not come close to the difference that simply getting them into school would make. This is harder work, and mostly not work for schools alone. But in a city with unremarkable deprivation and an absence problem at the very extreme, there is a great deal that can be done.

And the cost side of the scale is not empty, whatever the claim that "no one loses". Sending more children away from their nearest school means longer journeys, more time lost, more community frayed — and, above all, more school missed. The further a child travels, the more absence creeps up. That burden falls hardest on the poorest: not only in bus fares, an issue Class Divide have powerfully exposed, but because missing school damages disadvantaged children's results almost twice as much as everyone else's. In a city with our absence problem, at the very age when attendance starts to slide, that is the cost we can least afford.

Then there is fairness. It is often said that families in the central catchments enjoy an unfair choice of two schools. But choice depends on there being enough places to go round. Where a catchment has more children than seats — as was projected for the Stringer and Varndean catchment — choice evaporates for everyone in it. Under an oversubscription lottery, what those families have is not a choice but a chance: their name in a hat, a roll of the dice. Set that against a guaranteed place at a local school, plus a chance of one further afield should you want it. Which of those is fairer?

Some have suggested that for a professor to weigh in like this is a misuse of academic authority. I would say the opposite. Contributing to public debate on questions within your expertise is not a betrayal of the job; it is the job. And this work asks nobody to take my word for anything: it is built on data the Government publishes for every school in the country, with the methods open for anyone — the council, Class Divide, any other academic — to check, challenge or overturn.

So let me end with two questions. Is it fair that a child should be denied a place at their local school so that another child can gain a large educational benefit by travelling in? Reasonable people might well answer yes, and that is roughly the answer the council gave. But now ask the version the evidence actually puts to us: is it fair to deny that place when the benefit is unlikely to arrive at all? People will have their own views and they are entitled to them. What none of us should accept is being asked to hold an opinion — or vote on a policy — without being in full possession of the facts. For two years, we were. We are better placed now. Let the next conversation about our schools be the one we can finally have in the light.

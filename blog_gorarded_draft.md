**Suggested title:** *You've been Gorarded*

**Standfirst:** *On a clever way to appear to win an argument you are actually losing — and why openness is the only real answer to it.*

---

If you do quantitative research on anything people care about, sooner or later you meet a particular kind of criticism. It sounds technical, it sounds devastating, and to anyone who isn't a specialist it seems to end the conversation before it has begun. I met one recently, and it is worth explaining — not to settle a score, but because the technique is common, it is clever, and once you can see it, it loses its power.

First, some fairness. Professor Stephen Gorard is a serious and influential education academic, and I have no quarrel with much of his work — my own report uses his measure of school segregation, precisely because it is well established. Which is part of why this particular move is disappointing.

**The objection.** Professor Gorard has suggested that the statistics in my report are invalid. The argument runs like this: a "significance test" — the p-value beloved of researchers — only makes sense if your data come from a random sample. My data are not a sample at all; they are every school in England. So, he says, my significance tests are "mathematically meaningless."

Put that way, to a room of councillors deciding school policy, it lands like a knockout. *He is a professor; he says you have made a basic error; therefore everything you say is wrong and everything he says is right.* That is the move, and it is worth naming, because it substitutes authority for argument. Whether or not it is meant that way, the effect on a non-specialist audience is to belittle rather than to inform.

Here is why it does not hold.

**It is not really about my work.** Professor Gorard has been making this exact argument, in almost the same words, against most of his own discipline for about twenty years. In a 2016 paper he wrote that anyone who "continues to teach, use or publish significance tests is acting unethically," and called his colleagues' practice "dangerous obstinacy." That is not a critique of my report — it is a standing position against nearly everyone who does quantitative social science. An objection aimed at an entire field, whatever study happens to be in front of it, cannot tell you anything about any one study.

**His own field has answered him, repeatedly.** This is not settled science with Professor Gorard on the right side of it; it is a debate his peers have rejected in print, again and again. A Cambridge researcher, Dave Neale, wrote that Gorard presents a long-running, unsettled argument "as if the matter has been settled… and educational researchers simply need to 'come to their senses.'" Two academics at Durham — Gorard's *own university* — and Northumbria found that the very demonstration in his headline paper "uses a sample size which is far too small" and "misrepresents what hypothesis tests claim to do," and that his charge of unethical conduct "impugns the integrity, not just the competence, of a large number of highly respected academics." Two more, at the LSE, showed that the alternative method he offers in its place is itself flawed. Even the American Statistical Association — which agrees p-values are widely *misused* — does not say they are meaningless.

**If he were right, most of modern research would vanish.** This is the real tell. If inference on complete populations were genuinely meaningless, then most census-based social science, most economics using administrative data, and the whole of population health research using patient registries would be invalid too — the work of thousands of statisticians over decades. (Over half of all administrative-data studies in the leading US education journals use exactly the method he condemns.) You are asked to believe that they are all wrong and he alone is right. The likelier explanation is that the objection simply proves too much.

**And it changes nothing anyway.** Here is the part that gives the game away. None of my conclusions depend on a single p-value. They rest on the *size* of effects, measured in GCSE points; on the same result appearing in four separate years; and, above all, on the fact that my findings reproduce what education research has known since the 1960s — that a school accounts for only a small slice of how well its children do. Delete every significance test from my report and not one conclusion changes.

**A phrase worth returning.** Professor Gorard titled that 2016 paper "Damaging Real Lives Through Obstinacy." I would gently hand the phrase back. The claim he offered the council — that moving disadvantaged children between schools costs them nothing, that "no one loses" — is the one that risks damaging real lives: the children sent further from home, whose attendance and sense of belonging the evidence suggests will suffer for it. The obstinacy worth worrying about is not a discipline's careful use of a standard statistical tool. It is the insistence, against the evidence, that a policy with real and unequal costs has none.

**The antidote.** If I have learned one thing, it is that the answer to "trust me, I'm a professor" is never "no, trust *me*, I'm a professor." It is openness. Every number in my report comes from data the government publishes for every school in the country, and every method is there for anyone — Professor Gorard included — to check, rerun, or overturn. You do not have to take my word for any of it. That is the entire point.

So if someone ever tells you a piece of research can be dismissed on a technicality you are not equipped to judge, ask two questions. Does the objection apply to almost everyone, or only to this? And if it were true, would it quietly delete half of science? If the answers are yes and yes, then you have not been refuted. You have just been Gorarded.

## Counsel before you publish this

- **This is the most combative piece in the whole set, and it's aimed at a named, senior academic.** Everything in it is sourced and defensible — but a standalone public post whose whole purpose is to counter Gorard cuts against the "rise above / own the debate calmly" strategy that has worked so far. A reader sympathetic to neither of you may read a dedicated riposte as a sign of being rattled. My honest recommendation: **hold it in reserve**, or fold a much shorter version (the "does it apply to everyone / would it delete half of science" test, plus the openness point) into a broader piece, rather than lead with it. Deploy the full thing only if Gorard's critique is being used publicly against you in a way that needs answering.
- **The intent claim is deliberately hedged** ("whether or not it is meant that way, the effect… is to belittle"). Keep it that way — asserting he *consciously* set out to mislead councillors is the one line that could rebound, and you can't prove intent. The hedge lets the peers' words and his own words do the work.
- **The fairness opening is load-bearing.** The acknowledgement that he's a serious scholar whose segregation index you use is what stops this reading as a hit job. Don't cut it.
- **Every quotation is verified** against the PDFs in `Gorard_Papers/`: the "acting unethically"/"dangerous obstinacy" lines (Gorard 2016); Neale's "come to their senses"; Nicholson & McCusker's "too small"/"impugns the integrity"; Kuha & Sturgis on the flawed alternative; the ASA 2016 statement; Gibbs et al. (2017) for the "over half" figure.
- **If you run it as its own post:** rename to `blog2.qmd`, add the standard front-matter (title "You've been Gorarded", a one-line description, today's date), and drop this counsel section. It's ~950 words — a good blog length.

**Suggested headline:** *We argued about our schools in the dark for two years. Now we have the evidence — and it changes the question.*

**Standfirst:** *Adam Dennett is a Brighton parent and Professor of Urban Analytics at UCL. His new report analyses the Government's open data on every school in England.*

---

For two years this city tore itself apart over secondary school admissions — who goes where, who gets a say, who is left out. The arguments were passionate and sincerely meant, on every side, and I was part of them. But they were largely conducted in the dark. The evidence that could have answered the very questions we were fighting over was never really on the table. That has now changed — and it should change the conversation we have next.

Using the Government's own open data on every school in England, you can model what actually shapes how well a child does. The finding is a humbling one. The school a child attends explains only a small slice of the result; most of it is set long before the gates, by family, by poverty, and by the neighbourhood a child grows up in. This is not new: researchers have found it again and again since the landmark Coleman report in 1960s America and *Fifteen Thousand Hours*, the famous study of London schools, in 1970s Britain. Schools matter, and good ones matter a great deal — but far less than our league tables pretend. Which means the golden ticket at the heart of our admissions wars — the belief that getting your child into the "better" school across town will transform their chances — is largely a mirage. And campaigners in this city were right, too, that for families without means "choice" was never really a choice at all.

So let me put my case as plainly and as fairly as I can, because it is a moral argument before it is a statistical one. Two things were promised for these reforms: that they would be fairer, and that they would lift the results of our poorest children. Those are different claims, and they must be judged separately. Whether a child's school should hang on their postcode is a question of fairness and values — a real and serious one, which I would never presume to settle with a spreadsheet. But the promise that moving children between schools will raise the results of the disadvantaged is an evidence claim — and the evidence does not support it. When a policy imposes a certain cost on some children in pursuit of a benefit the best evidence says is unlikely, and when a better road to the very same goal lies open, the wise and decent course is to take that other road. Make the case for fairness honestly, if that is the case to be made. But we should not sell our poorest families an uncertain benefit to justify a certain cost.

And the cost here is not abstract. A policy that by design sends more children away from their nearest school means more children travelling further every day — and the evidence is clear that the further a child travels, the more school they miss, with the burden heaviest on the poorest, the very families whose bus fares Class Divide have so powerfully exposed. In a city with close to the worst absence in England, at the age when attendance already starts to slide, that is the one thing we can least afford to make worse. I do not say this to reopen the fight — the reforms are in place and rightly being evaluated. But that evaluation must ask one question above all others: are the children who, as a result of these reforms, now travel further to school missing more of it? That is the measurement that matters most, and it should be at the heart of the review. Because absence does such damage, and any benefit from mixing is so slight, that even a small rise in absence among those children could cancel the whole of it out.

Let me be clear where I agree, because it is most of the ground. Poverty is the root of the gap. In parts of our city far too few children leave school with a firm grasp of English and maths, and that is not acceptable. Brighton and Hove is already among the most socially mixed cities in England. And some of our schools serving the poorest communities are, once you account for the children they teach, among the very best in the country — and we almost never say so.

So if the golden ticket is a mirage, what is worth chasing? The evidence points to one answer above all others: attendance. It is the biggest thing our schools and our city can actually move, and it is where our real problem lies. A child who is not in the room cannot be taught. And absence is not, as some have called it, an unfixable excuse — the campaigners' own work shows exactly why our poorest children miss school: the bus pass a family cannot afford, the special needs that go unmet, illness in cold and damp homes. None of that is fixed by moving children around a map. All of it is a decision away from being fixed.

That is the challenge I want to leave with the city — and this time, we do not have to meet it in the dark. We have the data now. I would challenge the council to treat our absence figures as the emergency they are: to help the poorest with the cost of the journey, to fund the support that keeps anxious children in class, and to work with the communities who understand these barriers better than any spreadsheet. And I would challenge all of us to set the old argument down and pull together on the thing the evidence says would help our poorest children most. Brighton and Hove likes to go first. Let's stop being near the bottom of England for something we can change — and let the next conversation about our schools be the one we should have had all along: led by the evidence, and aimed squarely at the children who need us most.

## Notes / things to check before submitting

- **The spine is now the moral argument** (fairness vs attainment; certain cost vs unlikely benefit; "don't sell an uncertain benefit to justify a certain cost"), framed by *"we argued in the dark; the evidence now exists; the conversation should change."* That frame is your report's actual thesis (data existed but wasn't turned into usable intelligence for the consultation) — defensible and constructive, not a personal attack on anyone.
- **"Argued in the dark" / "evidence never on the table"** — a mild criticism of the *process*, not of people. Fair, and it justifies why you're speaking now. If you want it softer, "without the full picture" reads gentler than "in the dark."
- **Absence stat:** "close to the worst" is a safe placeholder — confirm the current figure/year (2024-25) and restore "second worst last year" if it stands up. The Argus will fact-check.
- **"The evidence does not support it"** — the strong claim. It's defensible (your models + the research tradition), but be ready to say *what* evidence and how strong if challenged. The framing as an *ex ante* decision (certain cost vs unlikely benefit) is what keeps it from being the "premature verdict" Class Divide warned against.
- **Fairness concession is load-bearing** — the moral argument only works because it concedes fairness is a legitimate values question the evidence can't settle. Don't let an edit erode it.
- **Class Divide credited twice** (bus fares / transport research; the reasons children miss school). Keep both — they turn opponents' strongest work into support for your point.
- **Whitehawk / BACA kept general** (no numbers, no school named). Decide if you want to name either.
- **Length ~810 words** — usable for a comment slot, though the Argus may still want 600–700; the concessions paragraph and one "I would challenge…" clause are the easiest further trims.
- **Tone:** concedes early and often, credits opponents, does not re-fight admissions or call anyone inconsistent, and lands on a uniting challenge. Read it aloud in your own voice, and — given how closely this will be read locally — show it to one neutral reader for the "generous or point-scoring?" test before submitting.

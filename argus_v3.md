# Argus opinion piece — draft v3 (alternative framing: cost–benefit scales + chance-not-choice)

**Draft for The Argus (education / comment). ~830 words. For Adam Dennett's review and adaptation — check the flagged facts before submitting (see notes at the bottom). v3 is a fresh reframing, not an edit of v7. The spine here is the question from the talk — *do the benefits outweigh the costs?* — treated as a literal set of scales: the reform only balances if the benefit on one side is big enough to justify the cost on the other, and the evidence now shows the benefit is small and the cost is real. It then pushes back on the "fairness and choice" framing with a *chance, not choice* argument, and closes on the two fairness questions and the fairness of simply having the facts.**

**Suggested headline:** *Do the benefits outweigh the costs? On our schools, we finally have the evidence to ask.*

**Standfirst:** *Adam Dennett is a Brighton parent and Professor of Urban Analytics at UCL. His new report analyses the Government's open data on every school in England.*

On Tuesday 21st July, I gave a public talk at BHASVIC about the drivers of secondary school attainment in England and zoomed in on our city's schools. This was motivated by two years of brusing consultations and controversial policies on secondary school admissions enacted in the city. The talk is available [here](https://adamdennett.github.io/school_attainment_tool/index.html), alongside the full report and accompanying analysis. At the beginning of the talk I posed a question; one that any policy like this has to answer: **Do the benefits outweigh the costs?**

It sounds a bit abstract, but it's not. When Brighton and Hove reformed secondary admissions, this is the judgement the council made implicit in the policy, even if not explicitly stated. On one side of the scales it placed a cost: some children would no longer get a place at their nearest catchment school. On the other it placed a benefit: some disadvantaged children would win the "golden ticket" — a place at a sought-after school in one of the central catchments that will boost their attainment outcomes. The council weighed the two and decided the benefit *was* worth the cost. In some ways this is a defensible way to make a decision. But a scale only balances if the weight on one side is genuinely heavy enough to justify the weight on the other. But the benefits were never quantified, despite the data being out there to do so, and the costs never fully acknowledged. So we never really knew if the benefits *did* outweigh the costs. 

My report gives us a better idea of the weights on either side of the scale - and while we can never predict the future with 100% accuracy, data at least gives us a better guess. Using the Government's open data on every school in England, you can model what really shapes how well a child does by the end of secondary school — and some of the findings are eye opening - although perhaps not for anyone familiar with educational research. The data reveals that the school a child attends explains only a small slice the GCSE scores they leave with (around 10-15%). Most of it is set long before the gates, by family, by poverty, by the neighbourhood a child grows up in. This is not a new or fringe idea: researchers have found it again and again since the landmark Coleman report in 1960s America and more recently in the field of School Effectiveness research in Britain. Good schools matter — but far less than our raw league tables imply. For schools with high headline GCSE scores (the schools that many conflate with being "good"), these scores are overwhelmingly to do with who they teach rather than what or how they teach. Conversely, there are a lot of very good schools whose students exceed expectations by some margin, but are often maligned due to low headline attainment scores. BACA in north east Brighton has been one such school, although I'm pleased to see perceptions are rapidly changing. This means the golden ticket is, for the most part, a mirage. Moving children between schools doesn't change who they are or where they are from - and this is what matters over and above everything else for their GCSE outcomes. The benefit on the heavy side of those scales is much lighter than the policy assumed.

To put it in plain numbers, the gap between disadvantaged children and the rest in England is about 12-15 points on Attainment 8, the score that adds up a child's GCSE grades. Even the most generous reading imaginable, plucking a child out of the "worst" school in the country and placing them in the "best", would close barely three-quarters of that gap. Moving children between schools turns out to be one of the weakest levers we have for attainment. My report puts the average gain from reshuffling pupils to even out disadvantage — the policy lever the council chose — at perhaps one to two Attainment 8 points, and, tellingly, *not* for the disadvantaged children it is meant to help (who, it turns out, tend to do better in schools with more disadvantaged children in). 

So what *can* be done to improve outcomes more significantly for disadvantaged students? The report reveals that by far and away the most important factor affecting attainment for disadvantaged students is absence. And last year, Brighton and Hove had the second worst levels of absence in the country. This is a city-wide problem and one faced by most schools. But lifting this city's attendance to the level of its own best-attended school would be worth around five GCSE points for disadvantaged childen and without moving anyone - a real lever where "no-one loses". Shuffling children between schools does not come close to the difference that simply attending any school regularly would make. Most of the gap was never about which school a child walks into. It is true that poverty sits at the root of the attainment gap, but its worst impacts are reduced by targeting the processes it operates through, like absence. Some have tried to wave my findings away as an "unfixable excuse" but I strongly refute that framing. This will take more effort to address and largely not through the schools themselves, but in a city with unremarkable levels of deprivation compared to other cities in England, but an absence problem at the extreme end, there are absolutely things than can be done. 

And the other side of the scale is not empty, one vocal proponent of shuffling childen between schools to to affect social mixing attainment is fond of suggesting that "no-one loses". That is a fallacy. Sending more children away from their nearest school means more children travelling further every day, more time lost, more community frayed — and, above all, more school missed. The evidence is clear that the further a child travels, the more absence creeps up. And the travel burden falls heaviest on the poorest, not just in the financial costs of bus fares - an issue Class Divide have so powerfully exposed - but missing school matters much more for disadvantaged children, impacting their attainment almost twice as much as those who are not known to be disadvantaged. In a city with close to the worst absence in England, at the very age when attendance starts to slide, that is the one cost we can least afford. There are other costs to community cohesion through pitting communities against each other and to trust in the council, too. The point is simply that the cost side of the scale is real.

This information makes those scales look a little different. But setting scales aside, another question emerges about whether the current arrangement of catchments, schools and access to them is 'fair'? When the current system of catchment areas was brought in in 2008 following the closure of CoMArt in the East of the City, given the density of children in the centre of the city, a system to ensure distance didn't determine access to schools while ensureing one of the two schools in a catchment didn't fill up at the expense of the other, was deemed the fairest solution at the time. Some have claimed that those living in the central catchments now have an unfair choice of two schools. But having a school choice depends on there being enough places to go round. Where a school is oversubscribed, where a catchment has more children than seats, choice evaporates for everyone in it. This was the council projected situation for families in the Stringer/Varndean catchment (those who are so often cast as the privileged choosers) this year and next. With the oversubscrption lottery that Brighton and Hove employs, what they have is not a choice but a *chance*: their name in a hat, a roll of the dice. The honest word for how places are shared out here is not *choice*, It is *chance*. And one that requires the use of two preferences in the hope you are allocated one local school. Contrast this with a guaranteed place at a local school in the outer catchments and a chance (possibly a very good one if others around you see the value in their local school) of a place in a central catchment, should you wish. Is *this* fair? 

Some have aimed criticisms at me rather than at the evidence — that for a professor to weigh in like this is a misuse of academic authority. I would put it exactly the other way around. Contributing to public debate on questions within your expertise is not a betrayal of the job; it *is* the job. Universities are funded, in part, precisely so that what we learn does not stay locked inside journals, and public engagement is written into what people like me are employed to do. And I have gone out of my way to do the opposite of pulling rank. This work asks no one to take my word for anything: it is built entirely on data the Government publishes for every school in the country, with the methods open for the council, for Class Divide, or for any other academic to check, challenge or overturn. That is not authority demanding deference — it is the reverse, an argument anyone can test for themselves. If I have it wrong, it will be easy to show, and I would welcome the correction. What I could not do in good conscience, as someone who works with exactly this data for a living, is stay silent about what it says. I would only say: watch the talk, read the paper, and make up your own mind.

I'll end with a different question. In a city that is already in the top quarter of Local Education Authorities in England for the integration of disadvantaged and non-disadvantaged students in its schools, in a city that has one of the very worse absence problems in England and where absence is the most important factor holding back the attainment of disadvantaged students, and where Class Divide have already highlighted the detrimental impact of longer distance travel to school on student well-being and the additional financial (and not to mention environmental) burden that creates, and where we know increased travel exacterbates absence. Is it fair that under the weight of this evidence, there are still those pushing for policies where one child should be denied a place at their local school because someone further away has been told through the policy decisions that have been made, that sending their child further away will benefit them? People will have their own views, and they are entitled to them. What none of us should accept is being asked to hold an opinion - or indeed take part in another consultation or vote on a another policy - without being in full possession of the facts. For two years we were. Now we are in a slightly better position, but there is more to be done. When know what absence is the biggest hindrance to Brighton and Hove being the very best education authority in the country for sending any child to secondary school (I may have forgetten to mention that we are right near the top). That, in the end, is the fairest thing this work can offer — and the next conversation about our schools should be the one we can finally have in the light.

## Notes / things to check before submitting

- **This is a distinct framing from v7.** v3's spine is the *scales* (benefit small × cost real → the balance no longer holds) plus *chance-not-choice*; v7's spine was *fairness vs attainment are different claims*. Pick whichever reads better in your voice — or lift the chance-not-choice paragraph into v7 if you prefer that as the base.
- **"Chance, not choice"** is the sharpest new move and the one most likely to be quoted. It's an argument about oversubscription, not a personal attack — but read it twice to be sure it lands as *reframing* rather than *point-scoring*. The claim is factual: oversubscribed catchments allocate by tie-break/distance, i.e. chance. Confirm that's an accurate description of the current mechanism before print.
- **Absence stat:** "close to the worst" is a safe placeholder — confirm the current figure/year (2024-25) and restore "second worst last year" if it stands up. The Argus will fact-check.
- **"The benefit is unlikely to materialise"** is the strong claim — defensible on your models plus the research tradition, but be ready to say *what* evidence and how strong. Framing it as an *ex ante* weighing (was the benefit ever big enough to justify the cost?) is what keeps it from reading as a premature verdict on the reform's results.
- **Fairness concession kept** — the two closing questions concede that a reasonable person could answer "yes" to the first. Don't let an edit turn this into "the council was simply wrong"; the force of the piece is that the *second* question is the real one, and it was never asked with the facts to hand.
- **Class Divide credited twice** (bus fares / transport; why children miss school). Keep both.
- **"Some have tried to wave my findings away as an excuse"** — mild, unnamed, and paired with "make up your own mind." Fine as is; drop the sentence entirely if you'd rather not engage at all.

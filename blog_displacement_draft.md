**Suggested title:** *It was never about who comes in*

**Standfirst:** *The objection to Brighton's admissions reforms keeps being misread — and the misreading is doing a great deal of work.*

---

There is a misunderstanding at the centre of the argument about our city's schools, and it is worth clearing up, because a great deal is being built on it.

When some parents objected to the admissions reforms, they were told — and are still being told — that what they were really objecting to was poorer children arriving in their schools. One of the academics brought in to review my report put it almost exactly that way: the only downside he could find was that "some parents in Brighton and Hove may feel concerned about their children having to attend with a refreshed intake." The campaign behind the reforms frames it the same way: wealthier families, reluctant to share.

That is not what was argued, and I think most people in this city already know it.

Brighton and Hove's schools are among the most socially mixed in the country. The families who objected already send their children to those mixed schools, alongside children from every kind of background, and are glad to. The objection was never about who might come *in*. It was about children being denied a place at their own local school — sent further across the city — on the promise that this would help someone else. That promise is what my report questions, and the evidence does not support it.

The distinction matters more than it looks. If children could come into a school from further away *without* displacing the local children who live beside it, there would be very little argument here at all. What people objected to was the displacement, not the mixing. Recasting a fair worry about your child losing their local school as snobbery about a "refreshed intake" does not answer that worry — it just changes the subject, and quietly puts the people raising it in the wrong.

I would add one thing about how this argument is being conducted, because it is relevant.

The academic criticisms of my report did not arrive out of the blue. By the campaign's own account, in a letter to councillors on the evening of the launch, they "asked a group of respected education professors to review" the report, and circulated the results. They are entitled to do that. But it is worth being clear-eyed about what it means: those reviews were commissioned and distributed by a campaign with a fixed position, as part of a lobbying effort — not offered as neutral scholarship. Which sits a little oddly beside the same letter's complaint that my report is not "peer-reviewed." (For what it is worth, a version is going through peer review now. But the honest answer is that the report is built entirely on data the government publishes for every school in England, with the workings open for anyone to check. You do not have to take a professor's word for any of it — mine, or anyone else's. That is rather the point.)

None of this is a reason to stop talking. I would genuinely welcome working with the campaign, the council and our schools on the thing we all agree matters most: getting more of our children into school. But we should at least be arguing about the right thing. And the right thing was never who comes in. It was the children being sent out.

- **Tone check:** this is firmer than the attendance piece but still constructive — it concedes the schools are mixed and welcome, credits the shared goal, and ends with an offer. It names the *tactic* (commissioned reviews) factually, using the campaign's own words, without impugning the academics' independence or anyone's motives. Keep it that way; the calm is what makes it land.

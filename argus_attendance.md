# Argus / The Conversation piece — attendance-led draft (warmer, plainer, move-the-conversation-on)

**A different kind of article, built to the new brief: lead with the unambiguous (second worst attendance in England), lead positive (how good our schools already are, and how much better the city could be), be generous to the campaigners, largely leave admissions alone except as the reason the digging started, shift the emphasis to the council, and end with a clear offer. Plainer language, shorter sentences, pitched for the Argus / The Conversation. Talk linked at the foot. ~760 words. Notes and fact-checks at the bottom.**

---

**Headline option A:** *Brighton could have the best schools in England for our poorest children. First we have to get them through the door.*

**Headline option B:** *The most important thing about our schools isn't who goes where. It's who isn't there at all.*

**Standfirst:** *Adam Dennett is a Brighton parent and Professor of Urban Analytics at UCL.*

---

Here is something that might surprise you. For a disadvantaged child, Brighton and Hove is one of the best places in England to go to school. Once you account for the children our schools actually teach, they are among the top handful of areas in the country for how far they carry their poorest pupils. Some of the schools that get talked down the most — BACA in the north-east of the city is one — are, on the evidence, doing some of the best work anywhere.

And yet we are also, quietly, near the bottom of the whole country for one thing. Last year, Brighton and Hove had the second worst school attendance in England. Not the second worst in the South East. The second worst in England.

I did not set out to write about attendance. Like a lot of people in the city, I spent two years pulled into the argument over school admissions — who gets into which school, and how. To try to understand it properly, I did what I do for a living: I went to the Government's open data on every school in the country and started to model what actually shapes how well a child does. I expected the story to be about which school a child attends. It wasn't. The same answer kept coming back, and it wasn't about admissions at all. It was about attendance. A child who is not in the room cannot be taught — and in this city, too many of our children are not in the room.

How much does it matter? More than almost anything else we argue about. If every school in Brighton got its attendance up to the level of our own best-attending school — not some impossible national target, a school here that already manages it — disadvantaged children would leave with around five more GCSE grade points each. That is a bigger prize than anything else on the table, and it is entirely ours to win.

I want to be careful here, because attendance is not a switch you flip. Children miss school for reasons that are mostly not their fault, and often not the school's either: illness, anxiety, special needs that go unmet, the cost of the bus, family life under real strain. As people who work closely on this keep reminding us, tackling absence is about [far more than getting children through the gates](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/tackling-absence-isnt-just-about-getting-pupils-through-the-gates/) — it is also about whether a child feels they belong once they are inside them. [One recent study that followed 200,000 pupils](https://www.impactedgroup.uk/news-and-blogs/what-is-driving-pupils-absence-from-englands-schools-61b72) found that children with a stronger sense of belonging attend more, that the slide often begins early — in the first year or two of secondary school — and that it falls hardest on disadvantaged children. Class Divide and other local campaigners have done the city a service in showing how heavily all of this bears on our poorest families. They are right about that, and they have been right to be loud about it. Everyone in this — the campaigners, the schools, the parents, the council — wants the same thing: the best for the city's children. Grasping attendance means taking those barriers seriously: health, transport, poverty, mental health, belonging. It does not mean lecturing families or blaming schools.

Which is why I think it is time to move the conversation on. The admissions argument grew out of a genuine wish to help disadvantaged children, and that instinct was the right one. But the evidence is now reasonably clear that moving children between schools does little for their results, while the disruption and the extra travel are real. Attendance is where that same energy would actually pay off.

And it is landing now. Attendance has become a national priority — every school in England now shares its daily registers, and it will be near the top of the agenda when the next inspection reports arrive in the autumn. The city has a chance to be ahead of this, not behind it.

None of this belongs to me. The data is open — the Government publishes it for every school in the country — and the tools I have built are there for anyone to use, and to argue with. A colleague put it well recently: academics are most useful when they share, support and partner, rather than pronounce from on high. That is the spirit I want to work in.

There is one thing we cannot yet answer, and I think we should. We know that children who live further from their school tend to [miss slightly more of it](https://ffteducationdatalab.org.uk/2023/11/are-pupils-who-live-further-away-from-their-school-absent-more-often/), and that this falls hardest on disadvantaged children in the GCSE years. We know, too, that children who feel they belong attend more. What we cannot yet say is what happens to a child's attendance, and their sense of belonging, when they are sent right across the city, away from their friends and their neighbourhood. It is a genuine open question — and exactly the kind a shared, open effort could finally settle.

So here is my offer. I would like to work with the council, with our schools, and with the community groups who understand these barriers best, to build one shared, honest, up-to-date picture of attendance across Brighton and Hove — where it is worst, who it hits hardest, and why — and to use it to get more of our children into school. We already have some of the best schools in the country for the children who need them most. Imagine what they could do if everyone turned up.

## Notes / strategy and fact-checks

- **Why this works (the strategic bet in the brief):** it refuses the fight everyone expects. By leading with attendance — the one thing neither the council nor Class Divide can argue against — and being warm about the schools and generous to the campaigners, you stop being "the admissions guy" and become the calm, data-driven voice who moved the debate somewhere useful. Admissions appears only as the reason you started digging, in one measured sentence, so there's very little for anyone to turn into a row.
- **The "first three paragraphs + last paragraph" rule:** the hook (paras 1–3) carries the whole story — best schools for the poor / second worst attendance / that's the real problem — and the last paragraph is the ask (a concrete partnership offer). A skim reader gets the point; a careful reader gets the argument.
- **Reading level:** deliberately plainer than v3 — short sentences, everyday words, no "lever", "mirage", "decomposition". Read it aloud; if any sentence needs a second breath, cut it in two.
- **Facts used, all verified:**
  - *Second worst attendance in England, last year* — 2024-25, behind Knowsley (school-mean of overall absence). **Cite the DfE local-authority absence release** as the source, not our panel, since DfE weights by pupil sessions; the ranking holds but the citable number is theirs.
  - *One of the best areas for disadvantaged pupils* — from your report's value-added caterpillar: **7th of 152 LEAs** for disadvantaged pupils, 4th for all pupils. If you want the hard number in, "seventh best in England" is strong and yours.
  - *BACA doing some of the best work* — its disadvantaged value-added was **+5 Att8 points last year (2024-25)**, top ~9% nationally. Keep it to "last year" (the multi-year average is lower).
  - *~5 more GCSE grade points if attendance matched the city's best-attended school* — best-attended state secondary is King's (6.6% absence); scenario gain ≈ 5.1 all / 5.6 disadvantaged, from your report's own model. Phrased plainly as "GCSE grade points" (Attainment 8 points).
  - *Moving children does little for results* — one sentence only, deliberately undramatic, no numbers, to avoid reopening the admissions fight. The detail lives in your report for anyone who follows the link.
- **Campaigners:** I credited "Class Divide and other local campaigners" generically. If **EIE** is a specific group you want to name and thank, add it explicitly — I left it out only because I don't want to misname it.
- **DfE "unintended consequences" sentence — REMOVED (was unsourced).** The claim that the DfE is asking schools to check pupil travel distance / out-of-community attendance could not be substantiated: the statutory *Working together to improve school attendance* (Aug 2024) does not mention travel distance/catchment as an attendance factor, and neither the Schools Week nor ImpactEd pieces contain it. Replaced with a defensible sentence (daily-register data-sharing is real; attendance is a genuine current Ofsted/DfE priority). If you later find a real source for the travel-distance point, it can go back in worded to match.
- **Two sources woven into the complexity paragraph (both solid):**
  - [Schools Week: "Tackling absence isn't just about getting pupils through the gates"](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/tackling-absence-isnt-just-about-getting-pupils-through-the-gates/) — supports "more than getting children through the gates / barriers beyond schools' control." Sector-credible, humane, matches your non-blaming stance.
  - [ImpactEd: "What is driving pupils' absence from England's schools?"](https://www.impactedgroup.uk/news-and-blogs/what-is-driving-pupils-absence-from-englands-schools-61b72) (200,000 pupils, 30,000 surveys) — supports belonging → attendance, the early-secondary (Y7→Y8) slide, and disadvantaged pupils hit hardest. Note: this is an evaluation-group blog, not peer-reviewed; strong and citable but describe it as "a study of 200,000 pupils," not "research proves."
- **The colleague quote** is paraphrased ("share, support and partner"). If it was a real person you can name or would rather not, adjust — as written it's anonymous and safe.
- **Open-question beat (new, just before the offer):** deliberately posed as a *question*, not a claim, to keep the piece above the admissions fight while still using the evidence. Anchored on [FFT Education Datalab (Nov 2023)](https://ffteducationdatalab.org.uk/2023/11/are-pupils-who-live-further-away-from-their-school-absent-more-often/): pupils living further from school are absent *slightly* more, the pattern is stronger for disadvantaged pupils, and most pronounced in Years 10–11. **Honesty discipline:** kept FFT's word "slightly" — the distance→absence effect is modest and associational at population level; the force comes from it landing on the disadvantaged, GCSE-age group where absence does most damage, not from the raw size. This is the England-wide, sector-trusted version of the Thomson (2023) finding your report already cites. The belonging half of the question is genuinely open (ImpactEd measured belonging but had no distance data) — hence "cannot yet say," which is true and sets up the offer.
- **The offer is the point of the piece.** Make sure it's an offer you actually want to make and can follow through on — a press reader (and the council) may take you up on it. If you'd rather ask the council to act rather than offer to partner, swap the last paragraph for a direct "the council should…" — but the partnership framing is warmer and harder to refuse.
- **One deliberate omission:** no "misuse of authority" rebuttal here. In a piece this constructive it would jar, and answering it in public raises it in public. The openness point ("none of this belongs to me… argue with it") does the same job quietly.
